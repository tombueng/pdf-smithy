# A page with remarks on it

This is the draft of the paragraph about the store roof, as it came back from
the second reader, and it has been left in the review with the marks still on
it so that anybody who has not seen a marked-up page can see what one looks
like. There are four kinds of mark here and each is a different thing.

A highlight is a wash of colour over words that are already there. It says
look at this, and it says nothing else. It does not change a letter of what
is underneath, and taking it off again leaves the page exactly as it was.

A note is a marker with a remark folded up inside it. The marker is small on
purpose. The remark can be as long as the reader likes, and it stays out of
the way of the words until somebody opens it.

A drawn line is a hand. Somebody has circled a figure or struck through a
line, and the line is theirs, in their own wobble, at whatever thickness they
were pressing. It carries information that a neat box does not.

A box is a boundary. It says this much, from here to here, and it is what to
reach for when the thing being marked is not a run of words but an area: a
picture, a table, an empty space where something ought to be.

## The paragraph as it stood

The store roof is original. The felt is at the end of its life and the
timbers below it are dry only because nothing has yet found a way through
them. Two people who know roofs have looked at it and said the same thing:
it will hold, and when it stops holding it will stop everywhere at once. The
trust has agreed to look at it in the spring. The estimate of four hundred
and forty is the yard's, given over the telephone, and should be treated as a
number somebody said rather than a number anybody has stood behind.

Marks live beside the page rather than in it. Every one of them can be taken
off and the paper underneath is untouched, which is the reason a marked-up
draft is safe to send to somebody who is going to typeset it.
