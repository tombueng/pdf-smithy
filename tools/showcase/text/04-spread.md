# The season in brief

Nineteen stays, forty-one visitors, one hundred and sixty-two nights slept
on the island. The boat crossed on eighty-eight days and was turned back by
weather on twenty-two, which is close to the ten-year average and a good deal
better than the two seasons before it. The generator ran for four hundred and
six hours. The rain gauge on the store roof collected six hundred and
eleven millimetres, of which nearly a fifth fell in the single week at the
end of the eighth month that everybody who was here will remember.

## Getting on and off

The landing wall was rebuilt in the first eleven days of the season by four
people, two of whom had never laid a stone before and one of whom had laid
nothing else for thirty years. The stone came from the old sheep fold above
the beach, which the trust had been meaning to take down since it bought the
island and which had been quietly falling down on its own in the meantime.
Nothing was brought over from the mainland except lime, sand and the loan of
a mixer.

The wall is now two courses higher than it was and set back a hand's breadth
further from the water, which sounds like nothing and is the whole
difference. A boat coming alongside at half tide in a westerly used to touch
the old face before the fender did. It no longer does.

What the rebuild cost the season is set out plainly in the table below, and
the reader should take the spring figures as incomplete rather than low.

## Weather

The station keeps three instruments: a rain gauge, a maximum and minimum
thermometer in a screen behind the store, and an anemometer on the mast that
has been repaired so often it should probably be described as a different
anemometer. None of them is certified. All of them are read at the same hour
every day by whoever is holding the log, and the value of that consistency is
worth more to the record than the accuracy of any one reading.

The season was warmer than the ten-year mean by about half a degree and
markedly wetter. The wet was not spread out. Four weeks of the season
accounted for more than half the rain, and the week in the eighth month
accounted for a fifth of it on its own. The gauge overflowed once, on the
Thursday, and the figure for that day is recorded as a minimum and marked as
such in the log.

Wind was unremarkable until the last fortnight, when a run of northerlies
kept the boat on the mainland for six days out of eight and gave the island
its quietest week of the year. Two visitors who had planned three nights
stayed nine. Both of them have asked to come back.

## The buildings

The bunkhouse is sound. The window on the seaward side was reglazed in the
fourth month after a stone came through it, which is the third time in six
years, and the trust has agreed that the fourth time will be answered with a
shutter rather than another pane.

The store is the problem, and it is the kind of problem that does not look
like one. The roof is original, the felt is beyond its life, and the timbers
underneath are still dry only because nothing has yet found a way through.
Two people who know roofs have looked at it and given the same opinion in
almost the same words: it will hold, and when it stops holding it will stop
everywhere at once. The trust has put it first on the list for the spring.

The boat house was painted, which is why there is a plate about paint later
in this review. The colour was chosen badly the first time, in a hurry, from
a chart held up against a wall on a bright day, and the result was a shade
that looked correct for about a fortnight and then looked like nothing on the
island at all. It was done again properly, against the rock, in the rain, and
the difference is the sort of thing that is impossible to argue about in
words and obvious in a photograph.

## The gauge in the sound

The tide gauge went in during the sixth week and has run without a break
since. It is a simple thing, and simple on purpose: a stilling well, a float,
a shaft, and a counter that writes a number to a card once a minute. There is
no radio in it. Somebody walks out to it once a fortnight, swaps the card,
and walks back. A drawing of how it works is included as one of the plates,
because more people ask about it than about anything else on the island and a
drawing answers the question faster than a paragraph does.

The reason it has no radio is not thrift. It is that the sound is a bad place
for a mast, the nearest thing to put one on is a rock that goes under at
spring tides, and a gauge that reports nothing for six hours twice a month is
worse than a gauge that reports everything a fortnight late. The card has
never failed. The card is not going to fail.

## Ringing

Totals are in the second plate, month by month. The short version is that the
spring is missing, the autumn is the best on record, and the difference
between those two facts is entirely the eleven days at the start.

Four visitors held licences this year, which is one more than last year and
the most the station has had at once. Two of them trained a third between
them across three separate stays, which is a slow way to train somebody and
the only way that works. The trust has agreed to keep paying the crossing for
trainees, and the warden would like it noted that this is the cheapest good
thing the trust does.

## Money

The trust is solvent, which for a body of this size means it has enough put
by to do one unplanned thing a year without asking anybody. This year the
unplanned thing was the wall. Next year it is going to be the roof, and the
roof is not going to leave room for anything else.

Membership stands at two hundred and thirty-one, up nine. Nobody has left.
Six members have been members since the trust was founded, and the warden
would like to thank all six of them by name, and has been told firmly that
this is not what a review is for.
